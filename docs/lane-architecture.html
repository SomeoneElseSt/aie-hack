SubQ-routed lanes for Pi · Splitwise-lite demo, 12 turns

Before and after: who reads what

The router saves money on small turns by moving their work to cheaper models. The first design lost that saving on the big turns. The fix keeps the expensive model's thread whole, which brought the session from 35% more expensive to break-even at the same quality.

The core trade, on one small turn ("ok, run the tests and commit")

Always the big model pays for

  • Writing the answer at $20/M output
  • Re-reading its whole long conversation on every request of the turn
→

With lanes, instead

  • A small model writes it at $0.20–4.25/M
  • over its own tiny thread
  • The big model later reads that turn's text once, as input at $4/M

Measured on the 7 small turns: $0.247 with the big model, $0.065 with lanes. About 74% less money, and 83% less on an average small turn.

Before: every lane isolated

Each lane saw only its own turns. The big model got terse scratchpad facts about the rest, so on each big turn it re-read files to get its bearings: twice the file reads and 2 to 5 times more tool output than the big model alone.

After: the big model keeps the whole conversation

Cheap lanes stay small and isolated, which is where the savings come from. The complex lane's thread is the full conversation: small turns are appended to its end, so its cached beginning never changes and it never has to re-orient.

Does it hold on Google? No.

Same 12 turns with Gemini Flash-Lite, Flash and Pro. Quality was equal (11 / 12 for every setup, all missing the same answer), but lanes cost more. Gemini Flash is only about 25% cheaper than Pro and took 49 to 81 requests where Pro needed 30 for the whole session. The router pays off only when the cheap models are much cheaper and about as efficient.
